What to Check Before Booking Building Services

25 November 2026

What to check before booking building services comes down to three things: the permissions the job needs, the builder’s credentials and insurance, and what is written down before work starts. This guide for UK homeowners takes each in turn, using the rules on GOV.UK and the advice Citizens Advice gives. The planning and building rules described are for England, with notes where the rest of the UK differs.

Quick answer: Before booking building services, check whether the job needs planning permission, building regulations approval or a party wall notice. Check the builder’s references, insurance and any scheme membership. Get at least three written quotes, agree a written contract and avoid paying everything up front.

Key Takeaways

  • Sort out permissions first: planning permission, building regulations approval and party wall notices are three separate things.

  • A trader on a competent person scheme can self-certify some work, such as windows, boilers and electrics.

  • Gas work must be done by a Gas Safe registered engineer.

  • A builder with employees must hold employers’ liability insurance. Ask to see public liability cover too.

  • Get three written quotes and a written contract, and pay in stages by card where you can.

What to Check Before Booking Building Services: Permissions First

Planning Permission

GOV.UK says you will probably need planning permission if you want to make a major change to your building, such as an extension, or change its use. Your local planning authority, reached through your council, can tell you whether your project needs it. If work that needs permission goes ahead without it, the council can serve an enforcement notice ordering you to undo the changes. For help picturing a project before you apply, see our guide on how to visualise a kitchen renovation.

Building Regulations Approval

Building regulations approval is separate from planning permission, and you might need both. In England the Building Regulations 2010 cover the construction and extension of buildings, and many alterations too, including replacing windows and doors, replacing a fuse box, installing a bathroom that involves plumbing and installing or replacing a heating system. Approval comes from a building control body, which is either your council’s building control department or a private registered building control approver. GOV.UK warns that without approval you will not have the certificates of compliance you may need when you sell. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules. In Scotland, permission for building work is called a building warrant and is granted by the local authority.

Competent Person Schemes

For some jobs you do not need to apply for approval yourself. An installer registered with a competent person scheme, for example for windows or boilers, can self-certify that the work meets building regulations. GOV.UK says they will give you a certificate within 8 weeks of completion, and that the schemes have insurance-backed warranties and complaints procedures. Citizens Advice notes that work such as an extension cannot be done this way and needs a building control body.

The Party Wall Act

In England and Wales you must tell your neighbours if you want to build on or at the boundary, work on a shared (party) wall or dig near their foundations. Examples include cutting into a party wall, removing chimneys from it, or making it taller, shorter or deeper. GOV.UK says you must give notice between 2 months and a year before you plan to start. You do not need to give notice for minor work such as plastering, rewiring or putting up shelves.

Checking the Builder: Schemes, Qualifications and Insurance

Trade Schemes and Qualifications

There is no licence for general builders, so look for evidence. TrustMark is the Government Endorsed Quality Scheme for work in and around the home, and the Federation of Master Builders is a trade association for building companies. Citizens Advice suggests asking for proof of qualifications, such as an NVQ in construction or a Construction Skills Certification Scheme card. Check any membership on the scheme’s own website and do not rely on a logo on a van.

Gas and Electrical Work

If the job includes gas work, you must use a Gas Safe registered engineer. For electrical work, Citizens Advice says to use a properly qualified or registered electrician, and you can search the Electrical Competent Person Register. Ask your builder who will do these parts of the job and check them yourself.

Insurance

Ask to see the builder’s insurance policies and check they will not run out before the work is finished. Public liability insurance covers injury to people and damage to property, including your neighbour’s. Employers’ liability insurance is a legal requirement for a trader with employees, and GOV.UK says the cover must be at least £5 million. Tell your own buildings insurer about the work before it starts.

References and Past Work

Recommendations from people you know are the best start. Otherwise ask for references and contact those customers yourself, because written references are not always genuine. Citizens Advice says to avoid traders who will not give references and to be wary of anyone who gives only a mobile number.

Quotes, Contracts and Payment

Get Written Quotes

Citizens Advice recommends written quotes from at least three traders. A quote is a fixed price and an estimate is a rough guess, so ask which you are getting. A good quote breaks down the work and materials and says when the price can go up, for example only if you agree to extra work. Be cautious if one price is much lower than the others.

Agree a Written Contract

Once you accept a quote you have a contract, whether or not it is written down, so get it in writing. It should cover timings, tidying up, materials, subcontractors and payment. Citizens Advice says you might be able to cancel within 14 days if you change your mind, though you may have to pay for work you asked to start in that time.

Paying for the Work

Aim to pay by card, pay in stages and avoid deposits or paying everything up front. Citizens Advice warns against traders who only take cash. Paying by credit card can give you extra protection under section 75 for work costing between £100.01 and £30,000.

Your Home During and After the Work

Health and Safety on Site

Under the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015, a homeowner is a domestic client. The Health and Safety Executive explains that the client duties normally pass to the contractor on a single-contractor job, or to the principal contractor where there is more than one. Agree with your builder how the site will be kept safe, who holds keys and where materials will be stored. Our home security tips for UK homeowners are worth reading while scaffolding is up.

Sign-off and Certificates

Walk round the finished job with the builder and write down anything that needs putting right before the final payment. Keep the building control and competent person certificates somewhere safe, because a buyer’s solicitor will ask for them. They sit alongside the other steps in our guide on how to prepare your home for a valuation.

Damp and Dust Afterwards

New plaster and concrete hold a lot of water, so ventilate well while they dry. If dark patches appear on walls later, our guide to black mould on walls explains the likely causes and what to do.

If Something Goes Wrong

Raise the problem with the builder in writing and keep a record. Citizens Advice points out that a trader has to carry out the work with reasonable care and skill, and if they do not you can ask them to redo it or refund some of the cost. If you paid by card, your card provider may be able to help. You can also complain to any scheme the builder belongs to and get help from the Citizens Advice consumer service.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I check before booking building services?

Check whether the work needs planning permission, building regulations approval or a party wall notice. Then check the builder’s references, insurance and scheme membership, get at least three written quotes and agree a written contract before work starts.

Do builders need a licence in the UK?

General builders do not need a licence. Gas work is different: you must use a Gas Safe registered engineer. For electrical work, use a registered electrician. Membership of TrustMark or a trade body such as the Federation of Master Builders is voluntary, so check it on the scheme’s own website.

Does my builder have to be insured?

A builder with employees must have employers’ liability insurance of at least £5 million. Public liability insurance is not a legal requirement, but you should ask to see it and check it will not run out before the work is finished.

Why are written quotes important for building work?

Citizens Advice explains that a quote is a fixed price and an estimate is a rough guess, so a written quote tells you what you will pay. It also gives you something to rely on if there is a dispute later.

What can I do if I have a problem with my builder?

Raise it with the builder in writing first. The work has to be done with reasonable care and skill, so you can ask for it to be redone or for some money back. If that fails, contact the Citizens Advice consumer service and any scheme the builder belongs to.
