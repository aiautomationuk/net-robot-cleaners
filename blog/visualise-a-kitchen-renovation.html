Visualise a Kitchen Renovation

10 November 2026

The easiest way to visualise a kitchen renovation before work starts is to build it in stages: an accurate measured plan, a layout you can test against how you cook, a 3D view, and real samples seen in your own light. Each step catches mistakes while they are still cheap to fix on paper.

Quick answer: Measure the room properly, draw it to scale, then test the layout against how you use the kitchen. Ask your designer for 3D views from where you will actually stand, and check worktop, door and tile samples in your own daylight before you sign off.

Key Takeaways

  • An accurate measured plan is the base for everything else, so get it right first.

  • Test the layout by walking through real tasks: unloading shopping, cooking, washing up.

  • Ask for 3D views from eye level, not just from above.

  • See finishes in your own room and light, at different times of day.

  • Lock the design before ordering, because changes after that are where costs creep in.

Why It Pays to Visualise a Kitchen Renovation First

A kitchen is one of the hardest rooms to picture from a flat drawing. Units have depth, doors swing, appliances need clearance, and a tall housing that looks fine on a plan can feel like a wall once it is built. Most regrets people describe afterwards are about things they could have spotted earlier: a corner unit that will not open fully, an island that blocks the path to the back door, or sockets in the wrong place.

Getting the picture right also helps you keep control of the budget. If you have read our guide to kitchen renovation costs in the UK, you will know the price moves with every change of layout, especially when plumbing, gas or electrics have to move. Deciding on paper is far cheaper than deciding with a fitter waiting.

Step 1: Start With an Accurate Measured Plan

Measure each wall at worktop height and at floor level, because older UK houses are rarely square. Mark windows, doors, radiators, the boiler, the soil pipe, the gas meter, existing sockets and switches, and the height of the window sill. Note which way doors open. Many kitchen companies will send someone to survey the room before final drawings, and it is worth waiting for that rather than ordering from your own tape measure.

If the job includes knocking through a wall or adding an extension, you will usually be working from an architect's or surveyor's drawings. Building regulations can apply to structural changes, drainage and electrics even when planning permission is not needed, so check with your local council's building control team before you commit to a layout that depends on it. Step 2: Test the Layout Against How You Live

Good kitchen layouts are built around tasks, not around how many units you can fit. Walk through a normal day on the plan with a pencil:

  • Where do bags of shopping land, and how far is it to the fridge?

  • Is there clear worktop on both sides of the hob and next to the sink?

  • Can the dishwasher door open while someone stands at the sink?

  • Will two people pass each other between the island and the units?

  • Where do the bins, the recycling and the ironing board live?

Low-tech tricks still work well. Masking tape on the floor marks out an island or peninsula so you can feel the walkway. Cardboard boxes stacked to worktop height show you how a run of units will sit against a window.

Step 3: Ask for 3D Views and a Walk-Through

Most kitchen designers now produce 3D images of the layout as standard. The most useful views are taken from eye level where you will actually stand: the doorway, the sink and the hob. A bird's-eye view looks tidy, but it hides how tall units and extractor hoods dominate a room.

What to check in the 3D views

  • Sightlines from the doorway and from any connected living space.

  • How much natural light reaches the worktop once wall units go up.

  • Whether handles, hinges and appliance doors clash in corners.

  • How the kitchen sits with the rest of an open-plan room.

For larger projects, an interactive model is worth more than a handful of still images, because you can move through the space and see it from any angle. If you are planning an extension as well as a new kitchen, a studio offering 3D modelling can turn your drawings into a model you can walk around on screen.

Step 4: See Real Materials in Your Own Light

Screens are poor at showing colour and texture. Ask for samples of door fronts, worktops, splashbacks and flooring, then look at them together in the kitchen in morning light, evening light and under the lights you plan to fit. A white that looks crisp in a showroom can turn grey or cream in a north-facing room.

Think about cleaning while you are choosing. Gloss doors show fingerprints, textured worktops can hold crumbs, and grout lines on a splashback need more upkeep than a single sheet of glass or stone. Matt finishes and handleless doors each have trade-offs, so handle the samples and ask how they are cleaned.

Step 5: Sign Off the Design Before Anything Is Ordered

Once you are happy, ask for a final set of drawings showing unit sizes, appliance models, socket and switch positions, lighting, and the finished floor and ceiling heights. Check it line by line against your measured plan. Changes after units are ordered or worktops templated usually cost time and money, so this is the point to be fussy.

Plan for the end of the job too. Building work leaves fine dust in cupboards, on light fittings and inside extractor hoods, and most people want a proper clean before they move back in. Our guide to what a deep clean includes explains what to ask for once the fitters have gone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need planning permission for a new kitchen?

Replacing a kitchen inside your home does not usually need planning permission, but an extension, a listed building or changes to the outside may. Building regulations can still apply to structural work, drainage, gas and electrics, so check with your council's building control team.

How accurate are 3D kitchen designs?

They are only as accurate as the measurements behind them. Use a proper survey of the room, and check that the 3D view uses the real sizes of your chosen units and appliances.

What is the cheapest way to visualise a kitchen renovation?

A scale drawing, masking tape on the floor and cardboard boxes at worktop height cost almost nothing and show you most layout problems. Add 3D views and samples once the layout works.
