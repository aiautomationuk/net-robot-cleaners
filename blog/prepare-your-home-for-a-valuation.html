Prepare Your Home for a Valuation

11 November 2026

To prepare your home for a valuation, make every room easy to reach and see, fix small obvious faults and have your paperwork ready. A valuer is judging the property, not your housekeeping, but a clean, accessible home lets them inspect properly and avoids guesses about things they cannot see.

Quick answer: Before a valuer visits, tidy and clean so floors, walls, windows and the loft hatch are easy to see and reach. Fix dripping taps and missing bulbs, open up any locked rooms or outbuildings, and gather certificates for extensions, windows, boilers and electrics. Point out improvements you have made.

Key Takeaways

  • A valuer looks at size, condition, layout, location and recent local sales.

  • Clutter does not lower the value, but it can hide things a valuer needs to see.

  • Small repairs show the home has been looked after.

  • Certificates and planning papers back up any work you have done.

  • Valuations for tax, probate or divorce follow stricter rules than an estate agent's appraisal.

What a Valuer Actually Looks At

A valuer compares your home with similar properties that have sold nearby, then adjusts for the things that make yours different. They measure or check the floor area, note the number and size of rooms, and look at the condition of the kitchen, bathrooms, windows, roof and heating.

They also note anything that could put a buyer or lender off, such as damp, cracking or an extension without approval. Furniture, paint colours and ornaments are not part of the figure, so there is no need for a show home. What matters is that nothing important is hidden.

Cleaning and Tidying to Prepare Your Home for a Valuation

Think of the valuer's route round the house. They will want to walk into every room, look at walls and ceilings, check under sinks, open the boiler cupboard and often look into the loft. Clear paths to each of these.

  • Clear worktops and floors so the size of each room is easy to judge.

  • Move boxes away from the loft hatch, boiler and meter cupboards.

  • Clean windows and open curtains and blinds to let light in.

  • Wipe away any mould around windows or in the bathroom so it can be judged fairly.

  • Cut back plants blocking paths, drains or the side of the house.

A thorough clean is not compulsory, but it helps the visit go faster. If you are short of time, our guide to what a deep clean includes explains what a one-off professional clean covers.

Small Repairs Worth Doing First

Little faults suggest bigger ones to anyone inspecting a house. A dripping tap, a cracked tile, a sticking door or a blown bulb are cheap to fix and remove questions. Bleed radiators, test smoke alarms and replace any missing sealant around the bath or shower.

Leave bigger projects alone unless you were planning them anyway. A rushed refit rarely adds as much as it costs, and the valuer will judge the finish. If you have recently done work, such as a new kitchen, mention it. Our article on kitchen renovation costs covers what goes into a refit if you are deciding whether to start one.

Paperwork to Have Ready

Documents help the valuer confirm what they see. Put these in one folder before the visit:

  • Planning permission or building regulations sign-off for extensions and loft conversions.

  • Certificates for replacement windows, boilers and electrical work.

  • Warranties for damp treatment, roofing or wall insulation.

  • The lease and service charge details if the home is leasehold.

  • A list of improvements with rough dates.

If a certificate is missing, say so rather than leaving it out. Your local council's building control team can advise on how to put things right for older work.

Different Types of Valuation

Not every valuation is the same. An estate agent's market appraisal is a free opinion on the asking price. A mortgage valuation is carried out for the lender to decide how much to lend, and it is not a survey of the building's condition. If you are selling, our moving house checklist covers the steps after the valuation.

Some situations call for a formal written valuation, usually from a RICS Registered Valuer working to the RICS valuation standards known as the Red Book. Common examples are probate and Inheritance Tax, Capital Gains Tax and divorce. To buy more shares in a shared ownership home, GOV.UK says you need a valuation by a surveyor registered with RICS. If the figure is needed for probate or a divorce settlement, RICS property valuations from Gibsons Surveyors in London and Essex are written for that purpose.

On the Day

Be around to let the valuer in and answer questions, then give them space to work. Have keys ready for garages, sheds and window locks. Keep pets in one room. If you know of local sales of similar homes, you can mention them, but the valuer will make their own judgement.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a property valuation visit take?

It depends on the size and type of home and the reason for the valuation. Ask the valuer when you book how long they expect to need so you can plan to be in. If you would like some help with this, get in touch with our team.

Does a messy house lower a valuation?

Clutter does not change the value itself, but it can stop the valuer seeing floors, walls or the loft. That may lead to cautious comments in the report, so clearing access is worth doing.

Should I redecorate before a valuation?

Usually not. Fresh paint over damp or cracks can raise questions later. Clean, make small repairs and leave larger decisions until you know the figure.

Is a mortgage valuation the same as a survey?

No. A mortgage valuation is for the lender. A survey, such as a RICS Level 2 or Level 3 report, looks at the building's condition for you as the buyer.
