Questions to Ask About Gates and Fencing

27 November 2026

The questions to ask about gates and fencing come down to five things: which material and style suits the job, whether you need planning permission, where the boundary is, what the quote includes, and how much upkeep to expect. Ask them before you accept a quote and you are far less likely to pay twice. This guide sets out each question and the answer to look for.

Quick answer: Ask which materials and styles suit your property, whether the height needs planning permission, who owns the boundary, what the written quote includes, what the guarantee covers and how much maintenance the fence or gate will need.

Key Takeaways

  • In England a fence or gate can usually be up to two metres high without planning permission, or one metre next to a road used by vehicles.

  • There is usually no official record of who owns a boundary fence, so speak to your neighbour first.

  • Get at least three written, itemised quotes and compare what each one includes.

  • New powered gates must be supplied with a Declaration of Conformity and user instructions.

  • Timber needs the most upkeep; composite, uPVC and aluminium need the least.

Questions to Ask About Gates and Fencing Before You Start

What Are the Main Types of Fencing and Gates?

Start by asking which styles the supplier fits and what each one is for. Closeboard (also called featheredge) and lap panels give privacy. Picket and trellis are lower and more decorative, while metal railings and palisade are chosen for security.

Gates follow the same pattern. A garden or side gate is mainly about privacy and looks, a driveway gate is about access, and a security gate is built to be hard to climb or force. Ask to see each option fitted at a home like yours.

Which Materials Suit Your Property?

Timber is the traditional choice and is easy to cut to an awkward opening, but it needs treating to keep rot away. Steel and wrought iron are strong and long-lasting, though they need a check for rust. Composite, uPVC and aluminium cost more at the start and need little more than a wash.

Ask how each material copes with wind and damp where you live, and ask to see samples outside at your own home. The same idea runs through our guide on how to visualise a kitchen renovation, which explains why real materials should be seen in your own light before you order.

How Should You Compare Styles?

Compare privacy, height, security and upkeep side by side. Solid closeboard blocks the view but catches the wind, so the posts matter more. Open styles let wind through and suit front gardens where a low boundary is all you need.

Ask for photos or addresses of work the installer finished a few years ago, so you can see how it has weathered. If you plan to sell, our guide on how to prepare your home for a valuation covers the small repairs worth doing first.

Wooden or Metal Gates?

Timber gates are easy to make to size and sit well in a garden, but they need staining or painting to stay sound. Metal gates are heavier and usually cost more to buy, and in return they hold their shape for longer. Composite and wood-effect gates aim for the look of timber with less upkeep.

Ask the supplier to quote for two materials on the same opening. Seeing the difference in price, weight and upkeep side by side makes the choice much easier.

Questions to Ask a Fencing Contractor or Gate Supplier

What Should You Ask Before Hiring?

Ask whether they hold public liability insurance and can show you the certificate. Ask how often they fit this type of fence or gate, and for references you can contact. Ask who will deal with planning questions and who takes the old fence away.

Ask what happens if they hit tree roots, pipes or cables when digging for posts, and what the guarantee covers. Our guide to the questions to ask about construction services goes through insurance, written quotes and contracts in more detail.

Does the Work Follow a British Standard?

BS 1722 is the British Standard series for fences, published by BSI. It comes in separate parts for different fence types, including chain link and steel palisade. Each part sets out how that type of fence should be made and installed.

A British Standard is not the law, so a fence does not have to follow it. You can still ask whether the installer works to the relevant part, and have that written into the quote.

What Should You Ask About Fitting a Gate?

Ask what the gate will hang on: timber, concrete or steel posts, or an existing wall or pillar. Ask how deep the posts will be set and how the ground will be levelled. Ask which hinges, latch and lock are included.

Slopes need extra thought. Ask how the gate will clear rising ground when it opens, and which way it will swing.

What Should You Ask About Powered Gates?

The Health and Safety Executive says new powered gates are covered by the Supply of Machinery (Safety) Regulations 2008, whether they are for a workplace or a home. The gate must come with a written Declaration of Conformity and full user instructions. Ask your installer for both before you pay the final bill.

Ask which safety devices are fitted to stop the gate if someone is in its path, and how often it should be serviced. The HSE strongly recommends that powered gates at private homes are checked regularly.

How Do You Know the Price Is Fair?

Get at least three written quotes for the same job. Check each one lists materials, labour, VAT and removal of the old fence, so you are comparing like with like. Ask what would cost extra, such as added height, gravel boards, post caps or a better lock.

Prices vary with the material, the length of the run, the ground and where you live, so a like-for-like quote tells you more than any average figure. Be wary of a price that is far below the others.

Planning Permission, Boundaries and the Rules

Do You Need Planning Permission?

In England, Planning Portal guidance says you can put up or alter a fence, wall or gate without planning permission if it is no more than one metre high next to a highway used by vehicles, or the footpath of one. Anywhere else the limit is two metres. If an existing fence is already taller, you cannot make it higher.

You will need to apply if your home is a listed building or shares a boundary with one, or if an article 4 direction or planning condition has removed the right. In a conservation area you might need permission to take a fence, wall or gate down. The guidance is for England, and it says policy in Wales may differ, so check with your local planning authority wherever you live.

Who Owns the Boundary?

GOV.UK says that in England and Wales there is usually no record of the exact boundary between two properties, or of who owns the fence, wall or hedge between them. Most title plans do not show exact boundaries. Your title plan from HM Land Registry will still give you a general idea.

Talk to your neighbour before any work starts. Agree where the fence will go, who is paying and who will look after it. If you both want it recorded, GOV.UK explains how to make a boundary agreement.

What Else Should You Check?

Ask before fixing anything to a wall or building you share with a neighbour. Check your deeds or lease for covenants too, as some estates limit the height or style of front boundaries.

What Problems Should You Look Out For?

Rot at the base of timber posts, rust on metal, leaning posts and panels loosened by storms are the usual faults. Most are easier to deal with when caught early. Walk the boundary after high winds and at least once a year, and fix small damage before it spreads.

Ask the installer what they do to prevent these problems, such as concrete or steel posts and gravel boards that keep timber off wet ground. Ask what the guarantee covers and how you would make a claim.

Are There Hidden Costs?

Ask whether removing and disposing of the old fence is included. Ask about extras such as gravel boards, decorative caps and locks, and have the ones you want written into the quote.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need planning permission for a fence or gate?

In England you usually do not, as long as it is no more than one metre high next to a road used by vehicles (or the footpath beside one) and no more than two metres high anywhere else. You will need to apply if the property is a listed building or borders one, or if an article 4 direction or planning condition has removed the right. Rules in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland may differ, so check with your local planning authority.

Who owns the fence between me and my neighbour?

GOV.UK says that in England and Wales there is usually no record of the exact boundary between two properties, or of who owns the fence, wall or hedge between them. Your title plan from HM Land Registry gives a general idea. Talk to your neighbour before any work starts, and consider a boundary agreement if you both want it recorded.

Can I put up a new fence or gate myself?

A simple timber fence or garden gate is a job many people do themselves. Heavy metal gates and anything powered are better left to an experienced installer. The planning limits apply whoever does the work.

What should I ask about ongoing maintenance?

Ask how often the fence or gate needs treating or painting, which products to use, and whether the installer offers a maintenance visit. Ask what would void the guarantee too.

What should I ask if I have pets or children?

Ask about the gaps under and between panels, the finished height, and how the gate latches and locks. A solid closeboard fence with a gravel board leaves fewer gaps than an open picket or trellis.
